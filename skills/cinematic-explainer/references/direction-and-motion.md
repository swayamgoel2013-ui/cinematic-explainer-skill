# Direction and motion

Use this reference to turn the explanation into shots, especially when a previous cut feels like a slideshow. Aim for a consistent film with a sequence of visible discoveries. The appropriate treatment may be playful, documentary, tactile, diagrammatic, photographic, or entirely typographic when the subject warrants it.

## A treatment that changes production decisions

Write a short treatment answering four questions:

- What does the main subject look and feel like?
- Which environments and camera views make its behavior understandable?
- What is the repeating visual connection between scenes?
- Which few moments deserve the strongest visual emphasis?

Specify a small material/color/type system and a few useful shot types. Avoid a long list of aesthetic adjectives without concrete staging. If redesigning, identify the observed failure first: tiny subjects, repeated layouts, disconnected captions, weak action, confusing scale, or an unsuitable theme. A palette swap cannot resolve a staging problem.

For example, a mechanism explainer might alternate an assembled product, a moving cutaway, a close contact point, and a slow resolved state. A software explainer might follow one piece of data through an interface, a process view, and the resulting user action. The film's subjects should determine its visual language.

## Plan events, not labels

A useful beat record has these fields:

| Field | Decision it captures |
|---|---|
| Start/end | The narration interval it serves |
| Idea | What the viewer should understand |
| Subject/state | What exists at the beginning and end |
| Visible action | The change the viewer can observe |
| Shot | Framing, scale, viewpoint, and camera behavior |
| Text | Only the label, value, or phrase that adds clarity |
| Constraint | A fact or continuity condition that must remain true |

A new row is not automatically a new shot. If only its title changes, the film may still be holding the same image. After authoring, compare the beat plan against the actual rendered events.

For an energetic short, a meaningful visual development every few seconds is a useful starting point, not a mandatory cut rate. Hold longer when a quantity evolves, a mechanism needs time to resolve, or the viewer must compare values. Give important results a moment of stillness.

## Three layers of motion

1. **Explanatory action:** the subject moves, transforms, accumulates, exchanges, reveals, or responds. This carries the meaning.
2. **Camera/edit:** a reveal, match cut, close insert, tracking shot, or change of viewpoint directs attention to the action.
3. **Surface life:** reflections, small ripples, glow, ambient particles, or secondary settling make the world feel finished.

Build in that order. If the explanatory action is absent, adding more surface life usually produces a busier still image. A small zoom on a complete raster scene is not a substitute for animating its subjects.

Use anticipation and follow-through where they help the viewer notice an event. Favor motion with a reason: follow a falling object, cut to the contact point, then show the result. Do not apply the same entrance bounce or camera drift to every scene.

## Composition and shot variety

- Give the active subject enough screen area to reveal the relevant detail. Use the upper frame for the subject when a headline is unnecessary.
- Alternate whole-system views with close inserts. Cropping an established apparatus is useful when the insert isolates the relevant part; accidental cropping of a required relationship is not.
- Make close shots unmistakably closer. A tiny scale change rarely creates a new visual idea.
- Use vertical space deliberately in a portrait film: a descent, a stacked process, a rising quantity, or a top-to-bottom reveal can explain well. Do not simply center a landscape diagram in a tall empty canvas.
- Keep text attached to what it names. A force value next to its arrow or a volume beside its vessel is easier to interpret than a duplicated headline far away.
- Reserve large type for a few earned phrases or quantities. Captions already carry the spoken sentence.
- Treat negative space as a choice. It can create emphasis or anticipation; long empty regions above a small subject often indicate an unfinished composition.
- Remove permanent branding or tiny technical furniture unless the requested deliverable needs it. Necessary conditions should be readable and near their subject.

At common portrait output sizes, test labels and captions at roughly phone display size rather than trusting the full-resolution preview. Choose sizes by actual legibility and density; avoid preserving a small label merely because it fits mathematically.

## Artwork and continuity

Use an identifiable subject across overview, close-up, and cutaway shots. Preserve meaningful proportions, landmarks, and colors so the viewer recognizes what they are seeing. A richly drawn product should not become an unrelated generic icon at the moment the explanation resolves.

For 2.5D artwork, a few coherent planes, bevels, occlusion, material highlights, and contact shadows can create more depth than many disconnected decorative details. Keep the illumination consistent. Layer the parts that must move independently.

Choose raster assets for texture, atmosphere, or compositions suited to a brief hold. Choose editable vectors, Canvas, 3D, or layered artwork for geometric changes and mechanisms. Use the user's preferred tools when suitable; do not invent tool availability or rely on an external generator being installed.

When making a cutaway, distinguish actual material, dry/empty space, and a measurement overlay. Hatching or an outline may clarify a virtual volume, but a label placed on a boundary can still imply the wrong state. Preserve the real subject through the reveal rather than swapping its identity.

## Correctness where it matters

Animate related quantities from one state model: a fill amount should control its level, counter, and corresponding geometry; a changed load should precede its visible consequence. Do not animate each element independently and hope their numbers agree.

For scientific or technical explanations, check the constraints the picture communicates, not only the script. Examples include force direction and attachment, conserved quantities, constant object size when demonstrating depth, appropriate units, and the distinction between a transient and a final equilibrium. For other subjects, apply the equivalent discipline to process order, data flow, dates, or interface behavior.

Simplification is useful when it preserves the point. State consequential conditions briefly and visibly, such as “schematic,” “at rest,” or “illustrative example.” Do not present synthetic visuals as empirical footage or create a false sense of measurement precision.
